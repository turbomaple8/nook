Sharing a Flat With International Flatmates: A Practical Guide

Four countries, one kitchen — why the mixed flat is London's best version of home.

Nearly half of London's co-living residents come from outside the UK, which means the typical shared flat is a small United Nations with one kettle. That mix is genuinely the best thing about London flatshare life — and it runs smoother when a few unspoken assumptions get spoken. Here's the practical version, no stereotypes required.

Why Mixed Flats Are the Good Ones

Ask people about their favourite London year and the story is usually a flat: the household where someone's homesickness cure became everyone's Sunday dinner, where you learned to make three cuisines and swear in four languages. An international flat is a built-in reason the city never feels closed — someone always knows a festival, a supermarket aisle, a film you'd never have found. You moved to London partly for the world; in a mixed flat, the world does the washing up next to you.

Where Assumptions Quietly Differ

The friction in international flats is almost never values — it's defaults. Everyone assumes their normal is the normal:

Kitchen hours and smells. Some of the world eats at six, some at ten; some cooking is fragrant and proud of it. The fix is ventilation and goodwill, not rules about food. (The flatmate whose cooking smells amazing is also the flatmate who might share it.)

Quiet hours. "Late" means 10pm in some households and 1am in others. Agree the flat's number out loud in week one — it's a thirty-second conversation.

Guests. Cultures differ on whether a cousin staying three nights is obviously fine or obviously a discussion. Make it a discussion by default; nobody's offended by being asked.

Shared versus labelled food. Some grew up with communal shelves, some with name-tags on eggs. Either system works — mixed systems don't. Pick one at the start.

Directness. The biggest one. In some cultures "the bins are overflowing" is neutral information; in others it's a confrontation. Agree early that flat-talk is logistics, not judgement — it licenses everyone to speak plainly.

The Three Agreements That Do the Work

1. The week-one conversation: quiet hours, guest habits, food system, cleaning rhythm. Fifteen minutes over tea — the same list as our 20 questions, just held after move-in instead of before.

2. A shared language for the flat. Whatever mix people speak socially, flat logistics happen where everyone's fluent — usually the group chat, in English, so nobody half-understands the plumber's visit.

3. Money kept boring. Bill-splitting across different banking habits and home currencies is friction nobody needs — the systems that work, or the all-inclusive route where there's simply nothing to split.

Stacking the Deck

The easiest international flat is one designed for arrivals: individual contracts (no one inherits anyone's rent), all bills inside one weekly price, and flatmates who are also new to London — so the "explain your normal" conversation is everyone's normal.
