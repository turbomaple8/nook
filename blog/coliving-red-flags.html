Co-living Red Flags: How to Spot a Badly Run House Before You Sign

The photos all look the same. The management doesn't. Here's how to tell before it's your problem.

Co-living's growth has a shadow side: alongside the well-run operators, London has houses where "co-living" means an overcrowded HMO with a lick of paint and an Instagram account. The listings look identical; the living experience is not. Here are the warning signs at each stage — and what the well-run version looks like, so you have the comparison in hand.

Red Flags in the Listing

No price, "DM for details". Transparent operators publish weekly prices and what's included. Hidden pricing usually means variable pricing — whatever they think you'll pay.

Suspiciously many rooms in one flat. Photos showing six bedroom doors off one corridor with a single kitchen suggests an overcrowded conversion. England's HMO licensing exists precisely for this; a legitimate operator can tell you the property's licence status without checking.

Stock photos energy. If the room photos have watermarks, mismatched lighting eras, or appear on three other listings, ask for a live video view of the actual room. (That request is your best all-purpose filter — the scam-pattern guide explains why.)

Red Flags at the Viewing

The current residents avoid eye contact. Happy houses are easy to read. If flatmates look weary or the host steers you away from them, that's data.

Locks in odd places. Padlocks on kitchen cupboards or fridges are the archaeology of unresolved conflict — a management that lets problems fester until residents fortify.

"The cleaner comes weekly" but the evidence disagrees. Run the standard 15-point viewing checklist; a well-run house passes it without the host improvising.

Red Flags in the Paperwork

Cash deposits, no scheme named. Deposits legally must sit in a government protection scheme, named in writing. No scheme = walk, whatever the room looks like. The deposit rules.

A "licence" where a tenancy belongs. Some operators hand non-lodger tenants "licence agreements" to dodge tenancy protections. If you're renting a room in a shared flat the landlord doesn't live in, you should generally be getting a proper tenancy — contract types explained.

Exit terms that only work one way. They can end your stay with two weeks' notice but you're locked for six months? Asymmetry in the exit clauses is the clearest single signal of how you'll be treated when something goes wrong.

What Good Looks Like (The Comparison)

Published all-in pricing. Named deposit scheme, one rent period held — at Nook that's four weeks, written into the contract. Individual tenancies. A repairs channel with a human on the end. Residents you're encouraged to meet at the viewing. Answers in writing to all ten sorting questions. None of this is exotic — it's the baseline, and any operator who treats baseline as premium is telling you their tier.
