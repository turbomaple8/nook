7 Co-living Myths, Debunked (By People Who Actually Live in It)

Half of what people believe about co-living was true in 2018 or never true at all.

Co-living has been mainstream in London for a decade, but the myths about it are older than most of the buildings. Some were true once; some were never true; a couple are half-true in ways worth understanding. Here are the seven we hear most, tested against 2026 reality.

Myth 1: "It's a dorm for adults"

The image: bunk beds, corridor bathrooms, noise. The reality: every room in mainstream London co-living is a private, lockable, furnished bedroom — at Nook, every listing is a private room in an ordinary shared flat. The dorm comparison fits some 500-room towers' aesthetics, but even there you're in a studio or private room. If you're picturing hostels, you're picturing the wrong product. What co-living actually is.

Myth 2: "It's only for extroverts"

The busiest social calendars in co-living are optional. In a shared-flat format, "community" mostly means three or four flatmates who say good morning and occasionally cook together — a home with people in it, not a programme. Plenty of residents are quiet people who simply didn't want to arrive in a new city to an empty room. A peaceful flat with polite near-strangers is a legitimate, common outcome.

Myth 3: "It's always more expensive than a flatshare"

Half-true, and the half matters. Against the cheapest DIY room you can hunt down, co-living usually carries a premium. Against the same room's true cost — bills, furniture if unfurnished, setup time, bad-flatmate risk — the gap shrinks and sometimes inverts. We did the full ledger in Is Co-living Worth It?; the honest answer is a break-even calculation, not a slogan.

Myth 4: "You can't choose who you live with"

You don't assemble the household — that's real. But you're not blindfolded either: viewings usually include meeting flatmates, operators can tell you the flat's mix before you book, and individual contracts mean moving rooms is an email rather than a legal event if it's not working. Compare that with a joint tenancy, where you chose your flatmates freely and are now financially chained to them. Both routes, honestly compared.

Myth 5: "The contracts trap you"

The opposite is the selling point. Mainstream London co-living runs on three-month-and-up terms precisely because its residents — interns, postgrads, relocators — have dates that move. The trap risk actually lives in panic-signed twelve-month private tenancies. Always check notice periods, but flexibility is the category's defining feature, not its weakness.

Myth 6: "Bills included means hidden catches"

A healthy scepticism, wrongly aimed. The catches live in vague listings — undefined "fair usage" caps, bolt-on bills packages. The fix is written specifics, which reputable operators provide as standard: at Nook, WiFi, electricity, water and council tax are named in the contract inside one weekly price from £215/week (subject to room availability), with a four-week deposit — one full rent period — protected in a government scheme. The decoder.

Myth 7: "It's a phase — you'll grow out of it"

Probably true, and that's fine. Co-living is optimised for a chapter: arriving, studying, first jobs, in-between years. Most residents graduate to solo flats or bought homes eventually. A product being right for two years of your life isn't a flaw — twelve-month tenancies don't apologise for being temporary either.

The Pattern

Every myth shrinks the same way: by replacing the image with the checklist. What's the room, what's included, what's the contract, what's protected. Run those four questions on any housing — co-living or otherwise — and marketing stops mattering. London's co-living options, compared honestly, or see Nook's rooms and judge the reality yourself.
